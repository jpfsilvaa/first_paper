\section{Computational Study\label{sec:experiments}}
We conduct a computational study to evaluate the performance of the proposed approach using real-world data from \hcunicamp. 
While the theoretical results provide guarantees on system stability, it is essential to assess the practical implications in a realistic setting.
To this end, we simulate the long-term surgery planning process and compare the proposed approach with a baseline policy in terms of patient waiting times and resource utilisation.

\subsection{Real-world Data\label{instances}}
The dataset comprises detailed information on surgery durations, operating theatre availability, team constraints, and specialty schedules over a one-year period (2025). 
Each month is treated as an individual instance of the OTSP, while the full year is used to evaluate long-term system behaviour.

\subsection{Derivation of Model Inputs}
The data are processed to derive the inputs required for the strategic and operational components of the proposed approach.

\paragraph{\textbf{Demand distributions}}
Patient arrivals are modelled as Poisson processes. 
For each subspecialty, the arrival rate $\lambda$ is estimated as the average monthly demand derived from historical activity.
This parameter is used in the Queue Management module to determine the $(R,Q)$ policy parameters, ensuring that waiting-time requirements are met with high probability despite demand uncertainty.

\paragraph{\textbf{Surgery duration distributions}}
For each subspecialty, empirical distributions of surgery durations are estimated from historical data. 
These distributions are used in the Overtime and Cancellation module to evaluate the newsvendor model and determine the number of surgeries scheduled per block; see Section \ref{sec:module2}.

\paragraph{\textbf{Operational parameters}}
All parameters of the operating theatre scheduling model (see Table~\ref{tab:symb} in Section~\ref{sec:ot}) are based on real-world data, i.e., the actual values observed in each month of the year 2025.
These include operating theatre compatibilities, staff availability, and working calendars.

\subsection{Experimental Setup\label{sec:experimental_setup}}
Experiments are conducted on a machine equipped with an Intel Core 7 150U processor (5.40 GHz) and 32 GB of RAM. 
All modules are implemented in Python 3.12, and the MILP model is solved using SCIP 9.2.3 with default parameter settings, interfaced through the Pyomo library.

For the Queue Management module, the waiting time target is set to $\waittimereq = 6$ months with confidence level $\conflevel = 0.1$, ensuring that at least 90\% of patients are treated within this horizon. 
The threshold $R$ is defined as the midpoint between $\lowq$ and $\upq$. 
In the Overtime and Cancellation module, the overtime cost is set to twice the regular surgery cost, \textcolor{red}{while the cancellation cost equals the regular cost ??}. 
Additionally, we set the cost function to be quadratic in the number of surgeries cancelled, i.e., $c(x) = x^2$ \textcolor{red}{(how about the value of g?)}, reflecting the increasing marginal cost of cancellations as more surgeries are cancelled. 
These strategic parameters (modules 1 and 2) are computed once and remain fixed throughout the simulation horizon.

\paragraph{\textbf{Baseline policy}}
The baseline policy excludes the strategic modules. 
All specialities are scheduled in every period, and the MILP model is solved using only operational constraints, without $(R,Q)$ control or overtime optimisation.
Essentially, we do so by deactivating the block demand constraints~\eqref{eq:c1} and~\eqref{eq:c2} in the MILP model and using the original surgery duration distributions (without adjustment for overtime or cancellations) to drawn the number of surgeries that happened into each scheduled block.
The patient arrival process and the operational parameters remain the same as in the proposed approach, ensuring a fair comparison.

\subsection{Simulation Framework}

Figure~\ref{fig:longterm_simulation} illustrates the simulation flow.
The system evolves over a monthly decision cycle.
In each period, patient arrivals are realised according to the estimated demand distributions, and queues are updated accordingly. 
Under the proposed approach, only specialties with queue size exceeding $R$ are selected for scheduling.

The MILP model then determines the allocation of operating theatre blocks. For the proposed approach, the number of surgeries is sampled based on the distributions obtained from the Overtime and Cancellation module. 
Queues are updated after the monthly cycle, and the process repeats at each new period.

This way, we can evaluate not only the expected performance but also the variability and robustness of the proposed approach compared to the baseline policy.

\subsubsection{Overtime and Cancellation Results\label{sec:overtime_results}}
The $(R,Q)$ results defined the target batch-size intervals required to regulate waiting-list levels across subspecialities. However, these targets must be integrated with block-level surgical plans before they can be used by the OTSP module. 
The overtime and cancellation module performs this planning by estimating, for each subspecialty, how many surgeries should be planned per block under uncertain surgical durations. 
This subsection reports the experimental results of this module, focusing on Foot Orthopaedics as a representative subspecialty, although the same analysis is performed for all subspecialities.

Figure~\ref{fig:newsvendor_pmf} shows the probability distribution of the number of surgeries completed in a Foot Orthopaedics block for different numbers of planned surgeries ($\plannedsurgs$). 
The distributions are computed after applying the newsvendor-based cancellation rule defined in Section~\ref{sec:cancel}, according to which an additional surgery is only started if the remaining session time is sufficiently large. 
%Thus, the figure represents the effective surgical output of a block, rather than its nominal planned workload.

\begin{figure}[htb]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.5\textwidth]{images/newsvendor_pmf_ort_pe_pmf.png}
    \caption{Probability distribution of completed surgeries in Foot Orthopaedics blocks for different planned values of $\plannedsurgs$.}
    \label{fig:newsvendor_pmf}
\end{figure}

The results show that conservative planning almost eliminates cancellation risk, but at the cost of low block productivity. Indeed, when a single surgery is assigned to a session ($\plannedsurgs=1$), it always goes ahead and therefore the probability of cancellations is zero.
When we allocate a single surgery in a session ($\plannedsurgs=1$), the planned workload is always completed, indicating that one surgery per block is a safe but inefficient decision for this subspecialty. 
For when we plan two surgeries ($\plannedsurgs=2$), the probability mass remains highly concentrated around two completed surgeries, suggesting that the block can usually absorb this workload without substantial cancellation risk. 
When planning three surgeries ($\plannedsurgs=3$), the distribution becomes more dispersed: completing all three surgeries remains feasible, but the probability of completing only two surgeries becomes relevant. 
This indicates that the third planned surgery increases expected throughput while also exposing the system to a non-negligible risk of cancellation. 
Finally, for $\plannedsurgs=4$, the probability of completing all planned surgeries becomes very low, and the realised output is more likely to remain below the nominal plan.

These results provide an important insight into the trade-off between productivity and cancellation risk.
The block does not behave as a deterministic capacity unit capable of accommodating a fixed number of surgeries with certainty. 
Instead, increasing $\plannedsurgs$ changes the probability distribution of realised surgical output. 
Therefore, using only nominal capacity would overestimate the contribution of a block when many surgeries are assigned to it. 
This is particularly relevant for the proposed framework, since the $(R,Q)$ policy may indicate the need to treat a certain number of patients in the month cycle, but the overtime module determines how much of this demand can realistically be assigned to each block without creating excessive cancellation risk.

Figure~\ref{fig:gain_cost} complements this result by comparing the expected marginal gain and the expected cancellation cost associated with each value of $\plannedsurgs$ for the same subspecialty. 
While Figure~\ref{fig:newsvendor_pmf} shows the probability of different realised outputs, Figure~\ref{fig:gain_cost} converts this uncertainty into a decision criterion for selecting the planned number of surgeries per block, as defined by the inventory-based model in Section~\ref{sec:patientsperblock}.

\begin{figure}[htb]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.5\textwidth]{images/gain_cost_ort_pe_gain_cost.png}
    \caption{Expected marginal gain and expected cancellation cost associated with planning $\plannedsurgs$ surgeries in a Foot Orthopaedics block.}
    \label{fig:gain_cost}
\end{figure}

The gain--cost comparison confirms that planning too few surgeries underuses available operating theatre capacity, whereas planning too many surgeries produces an unfavourable cancellation profile. 
For $\plannedsurgs=1$ and $\plannedsurgs=2$ (i.e., planning 1 or 2 surgeries), the expected gain is substantially higher than the expected cost, indicating that these planning levels are operationally safe. 
However, they are also conservative, especially when compared with the additional throughput that may be obtained by planning a third surgery. 
For $\plannedsurgs=3$ (i.e., planning 3 surgeries), the expected cancellation cost increases, but it remains below the expected gain. 
This suggests that the third surgery is still justified, since it improves expected block productivity without making the cancellation risk dominant.

The main change occurs when moving from $\plannedsurgs=3$ to $\plannedsurgs=4$ (i.e., planning 3 or 4 surgeries). 
At this point, the expected gain drops sharply, while the expected cancellation cost increases substantially. 
This pattern indicates that the fourth planned surgery contributes little to expected completed output and mainly increases the likelihood of cancellations. 
Accordingly, the experimental result for Foot Orthopaedics supports an optimal number of $\optplannedsurgs=3$ as the recommended number of surgeries to plan per block. 
This value represents the highest planning level for which the expected benefit of adding surgeries still compensates for the expected cancellation cost.

Thus, the overtime and cancellation results show that the effective capacity of each block is subspecialty-specific as it depends on the empirical distribution of surgical durations. 
For Foot Orthopaedics, as an example, the experiment indicates that three planned surgeries per block provide the best balance between productivity and cancellation risk. 
This output is then used by the operating theatre scheduling module after being combined with the $(R,Q)$ limits to determine how many blocks should be allocated to this subspecialty in each month.
Consequently, the MILP does not allocate blocks based only on nominal session availability, but on a probabilistic estimate of how many surgeries each allocated block can realistically deliver while controlling overtime-related cancellations.

\subsubsection{$(R,Q)$ Batch Size Results}
Figure~\ref{fig:rq_intervals} summarises the control intervals obtained by the $(R,Q)$ policy, converted to block units (see Section~\ref{sec:preprocess_otsp}), across all subspecialities. 
To improve readability, subspecialties are ordered according to the interval width, $\upq - \lowq$, rather than alphabetically. 
The figure should therefore be interpreted as an ordered profile of the allowed variation in the number of allocated blocks generated by the policy, while the complete set of limits is reported in Appendix~\ref{app:rq_limits} \textcolor{red}{Definir Apendice}.

\begin{figure}[htb]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.9\textwidth]{images/rq_blocks_interval.png}
    \caption{Control intervals for the $(R,Q)$ policy across subspecialties. It represents the admissible range of blocks that can be allocated to each subspecialty when its queue exceeds the reorder point $R$.}
    \label{fig:rq_intervals}
\end{figure}

The results reveal substantial heterogeneity across subspecialities. 
Although most services are associated with relatively narrow feasible intervals, a smaller group exhibits considerably higher lower and upper limits. 
As expected, this pattern is consistent with the variation in the patient demand distributions. 
Subspecialties with higher expected demands will trigger higher values of both $\lowq$ and $\upq$, whereas higher variances will be associated with wider intervals from $\lowq$ to $\upq$.

The high-interval subspecialities, such as ENT (Ear, Nose, Throat), neurosurgery, and vascular surgery, provide flexibility, as the number of patients released for scheduling can vary considerably from one planning cycle to another without compromising waiting time requirements. 
In a high-demand public hospital, this flexibility is important for though broad-access or high-referral subspecialities may accumulate patients rapidly when surgical capacity is constrained, they may also release significant surgical capacity to other specialities when their waiting times are low. 
A narrow or uniform release rule would therefore risk limiting the number of available allocation policies in the tactical scheduling model, as it would not be able to accommodate the variability in demand across subspecialties.

By contrast, the low-interval subspecialties operate within very tight allocation intervals. 
This does not imply lower clinical importance; rather, that their demand is smaller and more stable from the perspective of queue replenishment. 
The medium group occupies an intermediate position, representing services whose demand is recurrent enough to require enable some flexibility, but not sufficiently large or variable to dominate the release process. 
Overall, the $(R,Q)$ policy provides flexibility to the system by adapting the admissible replenishment range to the demand profile of each subspeciality.

These results have direct implications for CMP. 
As the $(R,Q)$ limits determine the number of patients transferred from the queue-management layer to the tactical planning stage, subspecialties with wider and higher intervals are more likely to influence capacity allocation and overtime requirements. 
This does not necessarily mean that these services should always receive more operating-theatre time.
In fact, it indicates that their larger and more variable demand must be explicitly represented in the tactical optimisation problem. 
Conversely, subspecialties with narrow intervals generate smaller and more predictable demand, reducing their impact on capacity allocation decisions.

Additionally, it is important to note that the $(R,Q)$ policy only triggers allocation decisions to a subspeciality when its queue size exceeds $R$. 
This means that even subspecialities with wider intervals will not necessarily dominate the block allocation process, as they will only be considered for scheduling when their queues are sufficiently large. 
This mechanism ensures that the system remains responsive and adaptive to actual demand conditions, rather than being driven solely by the potential variability in patient arrivals.

Therefore, the control intervals provide a quantitative link between queue dynamics and tactical planning. 
They translate differences in subspeciality-specific demand into structured release limits, ensuring that the scheduling modules are provided with an adaptive and flexible demand that is consistent with both the statistical behaviour of the queues and the operational characteristics of a high-demand public hospital such as \hcunicamp.

We analyse the evolution of patient queues over time and the allocation of operating theatre blocks, highlighting the differences between the two approaches.

\subsubsection{Queue Management Results}
The one-year simulation horizon enables a detailed characterisation of queue dynamics under both the proposed framework and the baseline policy. 
We analyse the results obtained from the $100$ independent simulation runs and capture the variability the 10--90\% percentile interval, allowing us to assess not only expected performance but also robustness under stochastic demand.

Figure~\ref{fig:queue_evolution_eda} presents the evolution of the queue for the subspecialty \textit{Upper Digestive Endoscopy (UDP) -- Pediatric}. 
Under the proposed approach (Figure~\ref{fig:eda_proposed}), the system exhibits two distinct operational regimes. 
Initially, a transient phase is observed, in which the queue decreases steadily from its initial level. 
This phase is followed by a controlled regime, where the queue starts to stabilise (approximately between months 6 and 9) and then fluctuates around the threshold $R$. 
This transition reflects the action of the $(R,Q)$ policy, which introduces a demand-driven mechanism: allocation decisions are triggered only when the queue exceeds $R$, thereby preventing unnecessary servicing once the backlog has been reduced. 
As a result, the system converges towards a stable operating point, with a stabilization in variability across simulation runs, as indicated by percentile band.

\subsubsection{Long-term OT Allocation Results}
This subsection analyses the long-term allocation decisions produced by the OT Scheduling module (see Section~\ref{sec:ot_scheduling}). 
While the $(R,Q)$ module defines when each subspecialty requires intervention and the overtime module estimates how many surgeries can be planned per allocated block, the OT Scheduling module converts these upstream decisions into a feasible monthly allocation of operating theatre capacity. 
Therefore, this module acts as the operational interface between queue-control decisions and theatre-level planning.

In the proposed framework, the MILP does not allocate individual patients directly. 
Instead, it assigns theatre blocks to subspecialties. 
For each allocated block, the expected number of planned surgeries is obtained from the overtime module, which accounts for the trade-off between overtime and cancellation risk. 
This modelling structure allows the MILP to operate at the same aggregation level used in practical theatre planning, while preserving the connection with patient-level demand through the queue-control policy.

We analyse the monthly operating room occupancy obtained under the proposed framework and the baseline policy in Figure~\ref{fig:room_occupancy}. 
The results show that the OT Scheduling module is able to generate feasible allocations with substantial use of available capacity in both approaches. 
This is consistent with the MILP formulation, since the model allocates blocks while respecting theatre availability, subspecialty compatibility, monthly capacity limits, and the surgical volumes determined by the upstream modules. 
Thus, the scheduling layer is able to translate the planned surgical demand into executable theatre allocations.

\begin{figure}[htb]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.9\textwidth]{images/room_occupancy.png}
    \caption{Monthly operating room occupancy under the proposed framework and the baseline policy.}
    \label{fig:room_occupancy}
\end{figure}

However, the baseline systematically presents higher occupancy than the proposed framework. 
This difference is expected and reflects the distinct logic behind the two allocation policies. 
The baseline allocates capacity without explicitly considering whether each subspecialty queue is above or below its reorder point. 
As a consequence, it tends to distribute theatre blocks across a larger set of subspecialties and maintains high occupancy even when some queues do not require immediate intervention.

By contrast, the proposed framework only triggers allocation for a subspecialty when its queue exceeds the corresponding reorder threshold $R$. 
If the queue is below this level, the framework does not force an allocation merely to use available theatre capacity. 
Therefore, the lower occupancy observed in some months should not be interpreted as a scheduling inefficiency. 
Rather, it indicates that the framework avoids unnecessary interventions when the queue-control policy identifies that a subspecialty is already within a controlled region. 
This behaviour is particularly visible in months such as July, May and October, where the proposed framework uses a smaller share of the available capacity than the baseline.

Figure~\ref{fig:specialties_allocated} complements this interpretation by showing the number of subspecialties allocated in each month. 
The baseline allocates a relatively stable and high number of subspecialties throughout the simulation horizon, ranging from approximately 34 to 39 subspecialties per month. 
In contrast, the proposed framework allocates fewer subspecialties in most months, with a more pronounced reduction in the second half of the year. 
This result confirms that the difference in occupancy is directly associated with the queue-control mechanism: when fewer subspecialties require intervention, fewer subspecialties are scheduled.

This behaviour has important implications for long-term planning. 
A policy that keeps allocating blocks to all or nearly all subspecialties may achieve higher occupancy, but it can also consume capacity on queues that are already under control. 
The proposed framework introduces a more selective allocation logic: capacity is concentrated on subspecialties whose queues require action, while subspecialties below $R$ can temporarily remain unallocated. 
This creates a controlled form of flexibility, because the MILP can redirect blocks across months according to the evolving state of the system rather than reproducing a fixed allocation pattern.

This controlled flexibility is a key distinction between the proposed framework and the baseline. 
The baseline tends to preserve a broader and more regular allocation of subspecialties, which leads to higher occupancy but weaker responsiveness to the actual state of the queues. 
The proposed framework, instead, links allocation decisions to queue status: it may increase, reduce, or temporarily suppress allocations depending on whether each subspecialty requires intervention. 
As a result, the framework does not simply aim to fill the operating room schedule; it aims to allocate theatre capacity where it contributes to maintaining queue stability.

Overall, the long-term allocation results show that the OT Scheduling module successfully implements the decisions produced by the queue-control and overtime modules. 
The occupancy results demonstrate that the MILP can generate feasible and operationally meaningful theatre plans. 
The lower occupancy observed under the proposed framework is consistent with the $(R,Q)$ policy, since capacity is not forced into subspecialties whose queues are already below the reorder threshold. 
The reduction in the number of allocated subspecialties confirms this selective behaviour, while the comparison with the $Q$ intervals shows that the framework preserves flexibility within controlled allocation bounds. 
Therefore, the proposed framework provides a planning mechanism that is not only feasible at the operating theatre level, but also more responsive to the long-term dynamics of surgical queues.
