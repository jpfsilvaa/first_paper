\section{Computational Study\label{sec:experiments}}
We conduct a computational study to evaluate the performance of the proposed approach using real-world data from \hcunicamp. 
While the theoretical results provide guarantees on system stability, it is essential to assess the practical implications in a realistic setting.
To this end, we simulate the long-term surgery planning process and compare the proposed approach with a baseline policy in terms of patient waiting times and resource utilisation.

\subsection{Real-world Data\label{instances}}
The dataset comprises detailed information on surgery durations, operating theatre availability, team constraints, and specialty schedules over a one-year period (2025). 
Each month is treated as an individual instance of the OTSP, while the full year is used to evaluate long-term system behaviour.

\subsection{Derivation of Model Inputs}
The data are processed to derive the inputs required for the strategic and operational components of the proposed approach.

\paragraph{\textbf{Demand distributions}}
Patient arrivals are modelled as Poisson processes. 
For each subspecialty, the arrival rate $\lambda$ is estimated as the average monthly demand derived from historical activity.
This parameter is used in the Queue Management module to determine the $(R,Q)$ policy parameters, ensuring that waiting-time requirements are met with high probability despite demand uncertainty.

\paragraph{\textbf{Surgery duration distributions}}
For each subspecialty, empirical distributions of surgery durations are estimated from historical data. 
These distributions are used in the Overtime and Cancellation module to evaluate the newsvendor model and determine the number of surgeries scheduled per block; see Section \ref{sec:module2}.

\paragraph{\textbf{Operational parameters}}
All parameters of the operating theatre scheduling model (see Table~\ref{tab:symb} in Section~\ref{sec:ot}) are based on real-world data, i.e., the actual values observed in each month of the year 2025.
These include operating theatre compatibilities, staff availability, and working calendars.

\subsection{Experimental Setup\label{sec:experimental_setup}}
Experiments are conducted on a machine equipped with an Intel Core 7 150U processor (5.40 GHz) and 32 GB of RAM. 
All modules are implemented in Python 3.12, and the MILP model is solved using SCIP 9.2.3 with default parameter settings, interfaced through the Pyomo library.

For the Queue Management module, the waiting time target is set to $\waittimereq = 6$ months with confidence level $\conflevel = 0.1$, ensuring that at least 90\% of patients are treated within this horizon. 
The threshold $R$ is defined as the midpoint between $\lowq$ and $\upq$. 
In the Overtime and Cancellation module, the overtime cost is set to twice the regular surgery cost, \textcolor{red}{while the cancellation cost equals the regular cost ??}. 
Additionally, we set the cost function to be quadratic in the number of surgeries cancelled, i.e., $c(x) = x^2$ \textcolor{red}{(how about the value of g?)}, reflecting the increasing marginal cost of cancellations as more surgeries are cancelled. 
These strategic parameters (modules 1 and 2) are computed once and remain fixed throughout the simulation horizon.

\paragraph{\textbf{Baseline policy}}
The baseline policy excludes the strategic modules. 
All specialities are scheduled in every period, and the MILP model is solved using only operational constraints, without $(R,Q)$ control or overtime optimisation.
Essentially, we do so by deactivating the block demand constraints~\eqref{eq:c1} and~\eqref{eq:c2} in the MILP model and using the original surgery duration distributions (without adjustment for overtime or cancellations) to drawn the number of surgeries that happened into each scheduled block.
The patient arrival process and the operational parameters remain the same as in the proposed approach, ensuring a fair comparison.

\subsection{Simulation Framework}

Figure~\ref{fig:longterm_simulation} illustrates the simulation flow.
The system evolves over a monthly decision cycle.
In each period, patient arrivals are realised according to the estimated demand distributions, and queues are updated accordingly. 
Under the proposed approach, only specialties with queue size exceeding $R$ are selected for scheduling.

The MILP model then determines the allocation of operating theatre blocks. For the proposed approach, the number of surgeries is sampled based on the distributions obtained from the Overtime and Cancellation module. 
Queues are updated after the monthly cycle, and the process repeats at each new period.

This way, we can evaluate not only the expected performance but also the variability and robustness of the proposed approach compared to the baseline policy.

\subsection{Results from Strategic Decision-Making\label{sec:longterm_analysis}}
In this section, we report and analyse the results obtained from the strategic modules (i.e., the $(R,Q)$ policy and the Overtime and Cancellation).
These both modules are independent of each other, but the results produced by them are merged to guide the tactical scheduling decisions in the OT Scheduling module.
This separation impacts the execution time of the overall framework, since the strategic modules are computed once and their outputs are used as fixed inputs for the scheduling module, which is executed at each month of the simulation horizon.

We start the discussion by the results of the Overtime and Cancellation module, which determines the expected number of surgeries that can be planned in each block while controlling cancellation risk. 
Then, we present the results of the $(R,Q)$ policy, which defines the control intervals for each subspecialty.
However, we present these intervals already converted to block units (see Section~\ref{subsec:preprocess_otsp}), according to the expected number of surgeries per block obtained from the Overtime and Cancellation module.
By doing so, we compare the intervals across subspecialties in a common unit (blocks) that is directly relevant for the scheduling module, rather than in terms of patients.
Essentially, the number of patients that can be treated in a block depends on the subspecialty-specific surgery duration distributions and the cancellation rules defined by the Overtime and Cancellation module.


\subsubsection{Overtime and Cancellation Results\label{sec:overtime_results}}
This section reports the experimental results of the Overtime and Cancellation module focusing on Foot Orthopaedics as a representative subspecialty, although the same analysis is performed for all subspecialities (see Appendix~\ref{app:oc_results}).
Figure~\ref{fig:newsvendor_pmf} shows the probability distribution of the number of surgeries completed in a Foot Orthopaedics block for different numbers of planned surgeries. 
The distributions are computed after applying the newsvendor-based cancellation rule defined in Section~\ref{sec:cancel}, according to which an additional surgery is only started if the remaining session time is sufficiently large. 
%Thus, the figure represents the effective surgical output of a block, rather than its nominal planned workload.

\begin{figure}[htb]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.5\textwidth]{images/newsvendor_pmf_ort_pe_pmf.png}
    \caption{Probability distribution of completed surgeries in Foot Orthopaedics blocks for different planned values of $\plannedsurgs$.}
    \label{fig:newsvendor_pmf}
\end{figure}

The results show that conservative planning almost eliminates cancellation risk, but at the cost of low block productivity. 
Indeed, when a single surgery is assigned to a session ($\plannedsurgs=1$), it always goes ahead and therefore the probability of cancellations is zero.
Therefore, allocating a single surgery in a session ($\plannedsurgs=1$) is a safe, but it is an inefficient decision for this subspecialty. 
For when we plan two surgeries ($\plannedsurgs=2$), the probability mass remains highly concentrated around two completed surgeries, suggesting that the block can usually absorb this workload without substantial cancellation risk. 
When planning three surgeries ($\plannedsurgs=3$), the distribution becomes more dispersed: completing all three surgeries remains feasible, but the probability of completing only two surgeries becomes relevant. 
This indicates that the third planned surgery increases expected throughput while also exposing the system to a non-negligible risk of cancellation. 
Finally, for $\plannedsurgs=4$, the probability of completing all planned surgeries becomes very low, and the realised output is more likely to remain below the nominal plan.

These results provide an important insight into the trade-off between productivity and cancellation risk.
The block does not behave as a deterministic capacity unit capable of accommodating a fixed number of surgeries with certainty. 
Instead, increasing the number of planned surgeries changes the probability distribution of realised surgical output. 
Therefore, using only nominal capacity would overestimate the contribution of a block when many surgeries are assigned to it. 
This is particularly relevant for the proposed framework, since the $(R,Q)$ policy may indicate the need to treat a certain number of patients in the month cycle, but the overtime module determines how much of this demand can realistically be assigned to each block without creating excessive cancellation risk.

Figure~\ref{fig:gain_cost} complements this analysis by comparing the expected marginal gain and the expected cancellation cost associated with each value of $\plannedsurgs$ for the same subspecialty. 
While Figure~\ref{fig:newsvendor_pmf} shows the probability of different realised outputs, Figure~\ref{fig:gain_cost} converts this uncertainty into a decision criterion for selecting the planned number of surgeries per block, as defined by the inventory-based model in Section~\ref{sec:patientsperblock}.

\begin{figure}[htb]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.5\textwidth]{images/gain_cost_ort_pe_gain_cost.png}
    \caption{Expected marginal gain and expected cancellation cost associated with planning $\plannedsurgs$ surgeries in a Foot Orthopaedics block.}
    \label{fig:gain_cost}
\end{figure}

The gain--cost comparison confirms that planning too few surgeries underuses available OT capacity, whereas planning too many surgeries produces an unfavourable cancellation profile. 
For $\plannedsurgs=1$ and $\plannedsurgs=2$ (i.e., planning 1 or 2 surgeries), the expected gain is substantially higher than the expected cost, indicating that these planning levels are operationally safe. 
However, they are also conservative, especially when compared with the additional throughput that may be obtained by planning a third surgery. 
When we plan 3 surgeries, the expected cancellation cost increases, but it remains below the expected gain. 
This suggests that the third surgery is still justified, since it improves expected block productivity without making the cancellation risk dominant.

The main change occurs when moving from $\plannedsurgs=3$ to $\plannedsurgs=4$. 
At this point, the expected gain drops sharply, while the expected cancellation cost increases substantially. 
This pattern indicates that the fourth planned surgery contributes little to expected completed output and mainly increases the likelihood of cancellations. 
Consequently, the experimental result for Foot Orthopaedics supports an optimal number of $\optplannedsurgs=3$ as the recommended number of surgeries to plan per block. 
This value represents the highest planning level for which the expected benefit of adding surgeries still compensates for the expected cancellation cost.

Thus, the overtime and cancellation results show that the effective capacity of each block is subspecialty-specific as it depends on the empirical distribution of surgical durations. 
For Foot Orthopaedics, as an example, the experiment indicates that three planned surgeries per block provide the best balance between productivity and cancellation risk. 
This output is then combined with the $(R,Q)$ limits to determine how many blocks should be allocated to this subspecialty in each month.
Therefore, the MILP does not allocate blocks based only on nominal session availability, but on a probabilistic estimate of how many surgeries each allocated block can realistically deliver while controlling overtime-related cancellations.


\subsubsection{$(R,Q)$ Batch Size Results}
In this section, we report the control intervals obtained by the $(R,Q)$ policy for all subspecialties.
Figure~\ref{fig:rq_intervals} summarises these control intervals, already converted to block units (see Section~\ref{sec:preprocess_otsp}). 
To improve readability, subspecialties are ordered according to the interval width, $\upq - \lowq$, rather than alphabetically. 

\begin{figure}[htb]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.9\textwidth]{images/rq_blocks_interval.png}
    \caption{Control intervals for the $(R,Q)$ policy across subspecialties. It represents the admissible range of blocks that can be allocated to each subspecialty when its queue exceeds the reorder point $R$.}
    \label{fig:rq_intervals}
\end{figure}

The results reveal substantial heterogeneity across subspecialities. 
Although most services are associated with relatively narrow feasible intervals, a smaller group exhibits considerably higher lower and upper limits. 
As expected, this pattern is consistent with the variation in the patient demand distributions. 
Subspecialties with higher expected demands will trigger higher values of both $\lowq$ and $\upq$, whereas higher variances will be associated with wider intervals from $\lowq$ to $\upq$.

The high-interval subspecialities, such as ENT (Ear, Nose, Throat), NEURO (neurosurgery), and  VASC (vascular surgery), provide flexibility, as the number of blocks (and consequently the number of patients) released for scheduling can vary considerably from one planning cycle to another without compromising waiting time requirements. 
In a high-demand public hospital, this flexibility is important for broad-access or high-referral subspecialities, as they may accumulate patients rapidly when surgical capacity is constrained.
Moreover, they may also release significant surgical capacity to other specialities when their waiting times are low. 

By contrast, the low-interval subspecialties operate within very tight allocation intervals. 
This does not imply lower clinical importance; rather, that their demand is smaller and more stable from the perspective of queue replenishment. 
The medium group occupies an intermediate position, representing services whose demand is recurrent enough to require enable some flexibility, but not sufficiently large or variable to dominate the release process. 

These results have direct implications for CMP. 
As the $(R,Q)$ limits determine the number of patients transferred from the queue-management layer to the tactical planning stage, subspecialties with wider and higher intervals are more likely to influence capacity allocation and overtime requirements. 
This does not necessarily mean that these services should always receive more operating-theatre time.
In fact, it indicates that their larger and more variable demand must be explicitly represented in the tactical optimisation problem. 
Conversely, subspecialties with narrow intervals generate smaller and more predictable demand, reducing their impact on capacity allocation decisions.

Additionally, recall that the $(R,Q)$ policy only triggers allocation decisions to a subspeciality when its queue size exceeds $R$. 
This means that even subspecialities with wider intervals will not necessarily dominate the block allocation process, as they will only be considered for scheduling when their queues are sufficiently large. 
This mechanism ensures that the system remains responsive and adaptive to actual demand conditions, rather than being driven solely by the potential variability in patient arrivals.

Therefore, the control intervals provide a quantitative link between queue dynamics and tactical planning. 
They translate differences in subspeciality-specific demand into structured release limits, ensuring that the scheduling modules are provided with an adaptive and flexible demand that is consistent with both the statistical behaviour of the queues and the operational characteristics of a high-demand public hospital such as \hcunicamp.

\subsection{Long-term Stability Analysis\label{sec:otscheduling}}
In this section, we evaluate the long-term stability of the system under the proposed framework and compares it with the baseline policy. 
The analysis focuses on two complementary dimensions: the evolution of patient queues over time and the monthly allocation of operating theatre (OT) capacity. 
Together, these results allow us to assess whether the proposed framework not only reduces queues locally, but also supports stable and demand-responsive planning at the system level.

\subsubsection{Queue Management Results}
The one-year simulation horizon provides a detailed view of queue dynamics under both policies. 
Results are reported over with the 10--90\% percentile interval to characterise variability across the simulations.

Figure~\ref{fig:queue_evolution_eda} presents the evolution of the queue for the subspecialty \textit{Upper Digestive Endoscopy (UDP) -- Pediatric}. 
Under the proposed approach (Figure~\ref{fig:eda_proposed}), the system exhibits two distinct phases. 
Initially, a transient phase is observed, in which the queue decreases steadily from its initial level. 
This phase is followed by a controlled regime, where the queue starts to stabilise (approximately between months 6 and 9) and then fluctuates around the $R$ threshold. 
This transition reflects the action of the $(R,Q)$ policy, which introduces a demand-driven mechanism: allocation decisions are triggered only when the queue exceeds $R$, thereby preventing unnecessary servicing once the waiting-list has been reduced. 
As a result, the system converges towards a stable operating point, where queues are maintained within a controlled region that balances waiting time requirements with capacity constraints.

Under the proposed framework (Figure~\ref{fig:gast_proposed}), the initial waiting-list is reduced rapidly and the queue subsequently remains close to the reorder threshold. 
This indicates that the $(R,Q)$ mechanism adapts allocation decisions to the evolving state of every subspecialty while maintaining a consistent target operating region. 
The queue does not continue decreasing indefinitely; instead, it stabilises around $R$, which is the intended behaviour of a reorder-point policy that aims to regulate rather than eliminate queues.

For the baseline policy (Figure~\ref{fig:gast_baseline}), the queue also decreases over time. 
However, this local improvement should not be interpreted as evidence of system-wide stability. 
As the baseline does not prioritise subspecialties according to their queue status, it may allocate capacity to subspecialties whose queues are already manageable (e.g., subspecialties with more available capacity), while other subspecialties remain congested. 
Thus, the baseline can produce favourable trajectories for some queues while still generating inefficient allocation patterns at the system level. 
This distinction is important: analysing a single subspecialty in isolation may obscure whether the policy is effectively coordinating capacity across the full set of queues.

As a consequence, the waiting time requirements are met with high probability, as the queues are maintained within the limits defined by the $(R,Q)$ policy.
Specifically, the queues do not grow indefinitely, nor do they remain persistently above the reorder threshold.

Some subspecialties move towards lower queue levels, whereas so many others experience sustained growth and remain substantially above their reorder thresholds. 
This dispersion reveals the absence of a system-wide stabilising mechanism: the baseline may perform adequately for selected subspecialties, but it does not ensure convergence across the full portfolio of surgical queues. 
Even worse, the baseline may fail to meet waiting time requirements for many subspecialties, as their queues has no explicit control.

By driving queues towards a controlled operating region, limiting excessive waiting-list accumulation, and supporting demand-driven prioritisation, our mechanism provide meaningful long-term stability, which is a critical requirement for high-demand public hospitals.
In contrast, the baseline policy lacks an explicit feedback mechanism based on queue status, which may lead to no compliance with waiting time requirements, resulting in locally acceptable but globally inefficient outcomes.

This section analyses the long-term allocation decisions produced by the OT Scheduling module described in Section~\ref{sec:ot_scheduling}. 
Figure~\ref{fig:room_occupancy} reports monthly operating room occupancy under the proposed framework and the baseline policy. 
Both approaches generate feasible allocations with substantial use of available capacity. 
This is consistent with the MILP formulation, which maximizes block allocations while respecting theatre availability, subspecialty compatibility, monthly capacity limits
For the proposed framework, specifically, the scheduling layer is able to convert planned surgical demand into executable theatre allocations.
It means that the MILP successfully implements the strategic decisions produced by the queue-control and overtime modules, translating them into operationally meaningful theatre plans that can be executed in practice.

However, the baseline systematically presents higher occupancy than the proposed framework. 
This difference is expected and reflects the distinct logic behind the two allocation policies. 
The baseline allocates capacity without explicitly considering whether each subspecialty queue is above or below its reorder point. 
As a consequence, it tends to distribute theatre blocks across a larger set of subspecialties and maintains high occupancy even when some queues do not require immediate intervention.

By contrast, the proposed framework triggers allocation only when a subspecialty queue exceeds its reorder threshold $R$. 
When the queue is below this level, the framework does not force an allocation merely to increase theatre utilisation. 
Therefore, the lower occupancy observed in some months should not be interpreted as an inefficiency of the scheduling model. 
Rather, it reflects a deliberate control mechanism that avoids allocating capacity to subspecialties already operating within the desired region. 

Figure~\ref{fig:specialties_allocated} supports this interpretation by showing the number of subspecialties allocated each month. 
The baseline assigns blocks to a relatively stable and high number of subspecialties, ranging from approximately 34 to 39 throughout the simulation horizon. 
In contrast, the proposed framework allocates fewer subspecialties in most months, with a more pronounced reduction in the second half of the year. 
This confirms that the difference in occupancy is directly associated with the queue-control mechanism: as more queues enter the controlled region, fewer subspecialties require active allocation.

    \caption{Number of subspecialties allocated per month under the proposed framework and the baseline policy.}

This behaviour has important implications for long-term planning. 
A policy that keeps allocating blocks to all, or nearly all, subspecialties may produce higher utilisation rates, but it can also consume capacity on queues that are already under control, while neglecting those that require intervention.
The proposed framework introduces a more selective allocation logic: capacity is concentrated on subspecialties whose queues require intervention, while subspecialties below $R$ can temporarily remain unallocated. 
This creates a controlled form of flexibility, allowing the MILP to redirect blocks over time according to the evolving state of the system rather than reproducing a fixed allocation pattern.

This distinction is central to the interpretation of the occupancy results. 
The baseline tends to preserve a regular allocation of subspecialties, leading to higher occupancy but weaker responsiveness to queue conditions. 
The proposed framework links allocation decisions to queue status: it may increase, reduce, or temporarily suppress allocations depending on whether each subspecialty requires intervention. 
As a result, the objective is not simply to fill the OT schedule, but to allocate theatre capacity where it contributes most to maintaining queue stability.

Lastly, it is important to note that the proposed framework aim to long-term stability rather than short-term occupancy maximisation. 
The scheduling module is designed to implement the strategic decisions produced by the queue-control and overtime modules, which are focused on regulating queues and managing cancellation risk. 
Therefore, the scheduling model may produce allocations that are not fully utilising available capacity in the short term, but that contribute to a more stable and responsive system in the long term.

Therefore, the long-term allocation results show that the OT Scheduling module successfully implements the decisions produced by the queue-control and overtime modules. 
The occupancy results demonstrate that the MILP generates feasible and operationally meaningful theatre plans. 
The lower occupancy observed under the proposed framework is consistent with the $(R,Q)$ policy, since capacity is not forced into subspecialties whose queues are already below the reorder threshold. 
The reduction in the number of allocated subspecialties further confirms this selective behaviour. 
Thus, the proposed framework provides a planning mechanism that is feasible at the operating theatre level and responsive to the long-term dynamics of surgical queues.
