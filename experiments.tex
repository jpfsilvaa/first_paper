\section{Computational Study\label{sec:experiments}}
We conduct a computational study to evaluate the performance of the proposed approach using real-world data from \hcunicamp. 
While the theoretical results provide guarantees on system stability, it is essential to assess the practical implications in a realistic setting.
To this end, we simulate the long-term surgery planning process and compare the proposed approach with a baseline policy in terms of patient waiting times and resource utilisation.

\subsection{Real-world Data\label{instances}}
The dataset comprises detailed information on surgery durations, operating theatre availability, team constraints, and specialty schedules over a one-year period (2025). 
Each month is treated as an individual instance of the OTSP, while the full year is used to evaluate long-term system behaviour.

\subsection{Derivation of Model Inputs}
The data are processed to derive the inputs required for the strategic and operational components of the proposed approach.

\paragraph{\textbf{Demand distributions}}
Patient arrivals are modelled as Poisson processes. 
For each subspecialty, the arrival rate $\lambda$ is estimated as the average monthly demand derived from historical activity.
This parameter is used in the Queue Management module to determine the $(R,Q)$ policy parameters, ensuring that waiting-time requirements are met with high probability despite demand uncertainty.

\paragraph{\textbf{Surgery duration distributions}}
For each subspecialty, empirical distributions of surgery durations are estimated from historical data. 
These distributions are used in the Overtime and Cancellation module to evaluate the newsvendor model and determine the number of surgeries scheduled per block; see Section \ref{sec:module2}.

\paragraph{\textbf{Operational parameters}}
All parameters of the operating theatre scheduling model (see Table~\ref{tab:symb} in Section~\ref{sec:ot}) are based on real-world data, i.e., the actual values observed in each month of the year 2025.
These include operating theatre compatibilities, staff availability, and working calendars.

\subsection{Experimental Setup\label{sec:experimental_setup}}
Experiments are conducted on a machine equipped with an Intel Core 7 150U processor (5.40 GHz) and 32 GB of RAM. 
All modules are implemented in Python 3.12, and the MILP model is solved using SCIP 9.2.3 with default parameter settings, interfaced through the Pyomo library.

For the Queue Management module, the waiting time target is set to $\waittimereq = 6$ months with confidence level $\conflevel = 0.1$, ensuring that patients are treated within this horizon with high probability. 
The threshold $R$ is defined as the midpoint between $\lowq$ and $\upq$. 
In the Overtime and Cancellation module, the overtime cost is set to twice the regular surgery cost, while the cancellation cost equals the regular cost. 
Additionally, we set the cost function to be quadratic in the number of surgeries cancelled, i.e., $c(x) = x^2$, reflecting the increasing marginal cost of cancellations as more surgeries are cancelled. 
These strategic parameters (modules 1 and 2) are computed once and remain fixed throughout the simulation horizon.

\paragraph{\textbf{Baseline policy}}
The baseline policy excludes the strategic modules. 
All specialities are scheduled in every period, and the MILP model is solved using only operational constraints, without $(R,Q)$ control or overtime optimisation.
Essentially, we do so by deactivating the block demand constraints~\eqref{eq:c1} and~\eqref{eq:c2} in the MILP model and using the original surgery duration distributions (without adjustment for overtime or cancellations) to drawn the number of surgeries that happened into each scheduled block.
The patient arrival process and the operational parameters remain the same as in the proposed approach, ensuring a fair comparison.

\subsection{Simulation Framework}

Figure~\ref{fig:longterm_simulation} illustrates the simulation flow.
The system evolves over a monthly decision cycle.
In each period, patient arrivals are realised according to the estimated demand distributions, and queues are updated accordingly. 
Under the proposed approach, only specialties with queue size exceeding $R$ are selected for scheduling.

The MILP model then determines the allocation of operating theatre blocks. For the proposed approach, the number of surgeries is sampled based on the distributions obtained from the Overtime and Cancellation module. 
Queues are updated after the month cycle, and the process repeats at each new period.

To assess long-term stability and the baseline, we perform $100$ independent simulation runs each, using different random seeds to capture the stochasticity in surgery durations and patient arrivals.
This way, we can evaluate not only the expected performance but also the variability and robustness of the proposed approach compared to the baseline policy.

Table~\ref{tab:rq_representative_intervals} illustrates how this heterogeneity translates into operational release limits. 
The high-interval subspecialties, such as ENT (Ear, Nose, Throat), neurosurgery, and vascular surgery, receive substantially larger control bands, indicating that the number of patients released for scheduling may vary considerably from one planning cycle to another. 
In a high-demand public hospital, this flexibility is important because broad-access or high-referral services may accumulate patients rapidly when surgical capacity is constrained. 
A narrow or uniform release rule would therefore risk limiting the candidate pool available to the downstream scheduling model.

By contrast, the low-interval subspecialties operate within very tight control bands. 
This does not imply lower clinical importance; rather, it indicates that their candidate pools are smaller and more stable from the perspective of queue replenishment. 
The medium group occupies an intermediate position, representing services whose demand is recurrent enough to require some flexibility, but not sufficiently large or variable to dominate the release process. 
Thus, the $(R,Q)$ policy avoids imposing a common batch size across the system and instead adapts the admissible replenishment range to the demand profile of each subspecialty.

These results have direct implications for CMP. 
As the $(R,Q)$ limits determine the pool of patients transferred from the queue-management layer to the tactical planning stages, subspecialties with wider and higher intervals are more likely to influence capacity allocation and overtime requirements. 
This does not necessarily mean that these services should always receive more operating-theatre time; rather, it indicates that their larger and more variable candidate pools must be explicitly represented in the tactical optimisation problem. 
Conversely, subspecialties with narrow intervals generate smaller and more predictable candidate sets, reducing their impact on capacity allocation decisions.

Additionally, it is important to note that the $(R,Q)$ policy only triggers allocation decisions when the queue size exceeds $R$. 
This means that even subspecialties with wider intervals will not necessarily dominate the block allocation process, as they will only be considered for scheduling when their queues are sufficiently large. 
This mechanism ensures that the system remains responsive to actual demand conditions, rather than being driven solely by the potential variability in patient arrivals.

Therefore, the control intervals provide a quantitative link between stochastic queue dynamics and deterministic tactical planning. 
They translate differences in subspecialty-specific demand into structured release limits, ensuring that the scheduling modules receive patient pools that are consistent with both the statistical behaviour of the queues and the operational characteristics of a high-demand public hospital such as \hcunicamp.


\subsubsection{Overtime and Cancellation Results\label{sec:overtime_results}}
The $(R,Q)$ results defined the target batch-size intervals required to regulate waiting-list levels across subspecialties. 
However, these targets must be integrated with block-level surgical plans before they can be used by the operating theatre scheduling model. 
The overtime and cancellation module performs this planning by estimating, for each subspecialty, how many surgeries should be planned per block under uncertain surgical durations. 
This subsection reports the experimental results of this module, focusing on Foot Orthopaedics as a representative subspecialty, although the same analysis is performed for all subspecialties.

Figure~\ref{fig:newsvendor_pmf} shows the probability distribution of the number of surgeries completed in a Foot Orthopaedics block for different planned values of $\plannedsurgs$. 
The distributions are computed after applying the newsvendor-based cancellation rule defined in Section~\ref{sec:cancel}, according to which an additional surgery is only started if the remaining session time is sufficiently large. 
Thus, the figure represents the effective surgical output of a block, rather than its nominal planned workload. \textcolor{red}{O tamanho da fonte desse grafico \ref{fig:newsvendor_pmf} (o seguinte tbm) ta discrepante com os outros, tem que ser ajustado.}

\begin{figure}[htb]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.75\textwidth]{images/newsvendor_pmf_ort_pe_pmf.png}
    \caption{Probability distribution of completed surgeries in Foot Orthopaedics blocks for different planned values of $\plannedsurgs$.}
    \label{fig:newsvendor_pmf}
\end{figure}

The results show that conservative planning almost eliminates cancellation risk, but at the cost of low block productivity. 
When $\plannedsurgs=1$, the planned workload is always completed, indicating that one surgery per block is a safe but inefficient decision for this subspecialty. 
For $\plannedsurgs=2$, the probability mass remains highly concentrated around two completed surgeries, suggesting that the block can usually absorb this workload without substantial cancellation risk. 
When $\plannedsurgs=3$, the distribution becomes more dispersed: completing all three surgeries remains feasible, but the probability of completing only two surgeries becomes relevant. 
This indicates that the third planned surgery increases expected throughput while also exposing the system to a non-negligible risk of cancellation. 
Finally, for $\plannedsurgs=4$, the probability of completing all planned surgeries becomes very low, and the realised output is more likely to remain below the nominal plan.

These results provide an important insight into the trade-off between productivity and cancellation risk.
The block does not behave as a deterministic capacity unit capable of accommodating a fixed number of surgeries with certainty. 
Instead, increasing $\plannedsurgs$ changes the probability distribution of realised surgical output. 
Therefore, using only nominal capacity would overestimate the contribution of a block when many surgeries are assigned to it. 
This is particularly relevant for the proposed framework, since the $(R,Q)$ policy may indicate the need to treat a certain number of patients in the month cycle, but the overtime module determines how much of this demand can realistically be assigned to each block without creating excessive cancellation risk.

Figure~\ref{fig:gain_cost} complements this result by comparing the expected marginal gain and the expected cancellation cost associated with each value of $\plannedsurgs$ for the same subspecialty. 
While Figure~\ref{fig:newsvendor_pmf} shows the probability of different realised outputs, Figure~\ref{fig:gain_cost} converts this uncertainty into a decision criterion for selecting the planned number of surgeries per block, as defined in Section~\ref{sec:patientsperblock}.

\begin{figure}[htb]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.75\textwidth]{images/gain_cost_ort_pe_gain_cost.png}
    \caption{Expected marginal gain and expected cancellation cost associated with planning $\plannedsurgs$ surgeries in a Foot Orthopaedics block.}
    \label{fig:gain_cost}
\end{figure}

The gain--cost comparison confirms that planning too few surgeries underuses available operating theatre capacity, whereas planning too many surgeries produces an unfavourable cancellation profile. 
For $\plannedsurgs=1$ and $\plannedsurgs=2$, the expected gain is substantially higher than the expected cost, indicating that these planning levels are operationally safe. 
However, they are also conservative, especially when compared with the additional throughput that may be obtained by planning a third surgery. 
For $\plannedsurgs=3$, the expected cancellation cost increases, but it remains below the expected gain. 
This suggests that the third surgery is still justified, since it improves expected block productivity without making the cancellation risk dominant.

The main change occurs when moving from $\plannedsurgs=3$ to $\plannedsurgs=4$. 
At this point, the expected gain drops sharply, while the expected cancellation cost increases substantially. 
This pattern indicates that the fourth planned surgery contributes little to expected completed output and mainly increases the likelihood of cancellations. 
Accordingly, the experimental result for Foot Orthopaedics supports $\optplannedsurgs=3$ as the recommended number of surgeries to plan per block. 
This value represents the highest planning level for which the expected benefit of adding surgeries still compensates for the expected cancellation cost.

The overtime and cancellation results show that the effective capacity of each block is subspecialty-specific as it depends on the empirical distribution of surgical durations. 
For Foot Orthopaedics, as an example, the experiment indicates that three planned surgeries per block provide the best balance between productivity and cancellation risk. 
This output is then used by the operating theatre scheduling module after being combined with the $(R,Q)$ limits to determine how many blocks should be allocated to this subspecialty in each month.
Consequently, the MILP does not allocate blocks based only on nominal session availability, but on a probabilistic estimate of how many surgeries each allocated block can realistically deliver while controlling overtime-related cancellations.

\subsection{Long-term Stability Analysis\label{sec:otscheduling}}
In this section, we evaluate the long-term stability of the system under the proposed approach and compare it with the baseline policy.
We analyse the evolution of patient queues over time and the allocation of operating theatre blocks, highlighting the differences between the two approaches.

\subsubsection{Queue Management Results}
The one-year simulation horizon enables a detailed characterisation of queue dynamics under both the proposed framework and the baseline policy. 
We analyse the results obtained from the $100$ independent simulation runs and capture the variability the 10--90\% percentile interval, allowing us to assess not only expected performance but also robustness under stochastic demand.

Figure~\ref{fig:queue_evolution_eda} presents the evolution of the queue for the subspecialty \textit{Upper Digestive Endoscopy (UDP) -- Pediatric}. 
Under the proposed approach (Figure~\ref{fig:eda_proposed}), the system exhibits two distinct operational regimes. 
Initially, a transient phase is observed, in which the queue decreases steadily from its initial level. 
This phase is followed by a controlled regime, where the queue stabilises and fluctuates around the threshold $R$ (approximately between months 6 and 9). 
This transition reflects the action of the $(R,Q)$ policy, which introduces a demand-driven allocation mechanism: allocation decisions are triggered only when the queue exceeds $R$, thereby preventing unnecessary servicing once the backlog has been reduced. 
As a result, the system converges towards a stable operating point, with a stabilization in variability across simulation runs, as indicated by the narrowing percentile band.

Although a short-term reduction is observed, the queue subsequently exhibits sustained growth and increasing dispersion. 
This behaviour indicates a misalignment between demand and capacity allocation, leading to backlog accumulation and reduced predictability. 
The widening variability further suggests that the system becomes increasingly sensitive to stochastic fluctuations, which may translate into unstable waiting times in practice.

A similar but more pronounced pattern is observed for the subspecialty \textit{Gastroenterology -- Esophagus-Stomach-Duodenum (ESD)} in Figure~\ref{fig:queue_evolution_gast_eed}. 
Under the proposed approach (Figure~\ref{fig:gast_proposed}), the initial depletion phase occurs more rapidly, reflecting the higher effective service capacity of this subspecialty. 
Subsequently, the queue converges to the same controlled regime around $R$, indicating that the policy adapts dynamically to differences in service rates while maintaining a consistent target operating level across subspecialties. 
This highlights that the $(R,Q)$ mechanism is not merely reactive, but also self-regulating, ensuring both convergence and stability irrespective of capacity heterogeneity.

For the baseline policy (Figure~\ref{fig:gast_baseline}), the queue also decreases over time due to the relatively high allocation frequency of this subspecialty. 
However, this apparent improvement is local rather than systemic. 
As the baseline seek to allocate all subspecialties uniformly, without accounting for their relative queue levels, it fails to prioritise resources where they are most needed. 
Consequently, capacity may be over-allocated to subspecialties with already manageable queues, while others remain congested. 
This imbalance is not visible when analysing a single queue in isolation but becomes critical at the system level, where it leads to inefficient resource utilisation and degraded overall performance.

These results have important implications for the integration of the queue management module with the subsequent components of the framework. 
First, by regulating queues around a target level, the $(R,Q)$ policy provides a stabilised and informative input to the overtime planning module, which determines the number of surgeries to be performed within each block. 
In particular, the reduced variability and bounded queue sizes lead to more reliable estimates of demand, thereby improving the calibration of safety margins and reducing the risk of either under-utilisation or excessive overtime.

Second, the stabilised queue profiles directly benefit the operating theatre scheduling module. 
By filtering out extreme fluctuations and preventing backlog accumulation, the queue management module effectively regularises the input to the scheduling problem, reducing the likelihood of infeasible or highly imbalanced allocations. 
This contributes to improved tractability and solution quality, as the MILP operates on a more balanced and predictable set of requirements across subspecialties.

The $(R,Q)$-based mechanism acts as a coordination layer between stochastic demand and deterministic scheduling decisions. 
By inducing convergence to a steady-state regime, reducing variability, and enabling demand-driven prioritisation, it enhances both the robustness and the efficiency of the downstream planning modules. 
In contrast, the baseline policy fails to provide such coordination, which may result in locally acceptable but globally inefficient solutions.
